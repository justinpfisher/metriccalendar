The short answers.

For exact rules and definitions, use the calendar specification. For a real date, use the converter.

What is the Metric Calendar?

A proposed ten-month calendar in which each month has six six-day metric weeks. Each metric week has four Focus Days and two Rest Days. Even months add a Bonus Rest Day, and leap years add one final day to month 10. The year still has 365 or 366 days.

Why ten months?

Ten gives the year a decimal grouping and five repeated pairs of months. It is a design choice, not evidence of an optimal number. The months are not all equal: they contain 36 or 37 days, with 38 in leap-year month 10.

Why a six-day metric week?

It expresses the proposed rhythm of four Focus Days followed by two Rest Days. The site does not establish that this cycle is healthier or more productive than a seven-day week. See the research and limitations.

Does the year still start on January 1?

Yes. Gregorian gYYYY-01-01 and Metric YYYY-01-01 are the same day. The year number stays the same, and the final Metric date is month 10, day 37 in a common year or day 38 in a leap year.

Is a Bonus Rest Day part of a month or a week?

It belongs to its month but not to a regular metric week. It is day 37 of months 02, 04, 06, 08, and 10. The next month begins at Focus Day 1. Gregorian weekdays keep running independently.

What happens to February 29?

It remains a day in the Gregorian year and converts by its ordinal position. For example, g2028-02-29 = 2028-02-24. Metric Leap Day is at the end of the year: 2028-10-38 = g2028-12-31. The two leap-day labels refer to different days.

Would metric Rest Days always fall on weekends?

No. The six-day metric cycle moves relative to the seven-day Gregorian cycle, and extra days sit between some metric weeks. This creates real coordination challenges for shared weekends, schools, care, and religious observance.

Is this the same as a four-day workweek?

No. Four working days within a seven-day week differ from four Focus Days in a six-day metric week. Metric Calendar has 240 designated Focus Days each year. Research on four-day workweeks does not directly test this arrangement. See the day-count comparison.

What about holidays and birthdays?

Keep the event’s original rule and convert each occurrence in its own year. A Gregorian holiday remains on its Gregorian date unless people explicitly decide otherwise. Leap years can change its Metric month/day. Religious calendars and weekday-based observances need their own original rules preserved.

Would salaries or hourly pay change?

The framework’s intention is to preserve salaried compensation, but a calendar cannot guarantee pay. Hourly earnings, overtime, leave, and coverage require explicit arrangements before a schedule trial. Read the adoption guidance.

How would essential services operate?

They would need service-specific staffing and rotating schedules. The proposal does not supply a tested roster, and labelling a day for rest does not remove the need for care, transport, emergency response, or utilities.

Has the Metric Calendar been proven to improve productivity?

No evidence presented on this site establishes that. The cited research studies other working-time arrangements. Productivity, well-being, and planning benefits for this calendar remain questions for direct evaluation.

Is immediate replacement of the Gregorian calendar proposed?

The practical starting point described here is a voluntary planning overlay with both dates shown. Civil calendar reform would require much broader research, public discussion, and coordination.

Does it change the clock or the length of a day?

No. The framework rearranges dates within the year. It does not change hours, minutes, or seconds. The converter handles dates only and performs no time-zone conversion.

Can I use the framework or suggest a correction?

The framework is shared for common public use with attribution to J. Fisher / MetricCalendar.org. The project page links to the source repository and explains the project’s scope.
