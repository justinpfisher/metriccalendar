Calendar specification

What is the Metric Calendar?

The Metric Calendar is a proposed ten-month calendar with six-day metric weeks, five annual Bonus Rest Days, and the Gregorian leap-year rule. It keeps the Gregorian year number and starts each year on the same day as Gregorian January 1.

Each month contains six complete metric weeks: 36 regular days. Days 1–4 of each metric week are Focus Days (also called Focus/Work Days); days 5–6 are Rest Days. In English, those days are named Begin, Create, Connect, Share, Pause and Renew respectively. These are designations in the proposal, not automatic changes to anyone’s employment schedule.

The defining equation

10 months × 6 metric weeks × 6 days = 360 regular days.
360 + 5 Bonus Rest Days = 365.
A leap year adds one Leap Day = 366.

This page states the rules used by the converter. The diagrams illustrate those rules; they do not introduce a different calendar model.

The ten metric months

Each metric month has a name alongside its numeric identifier, 01–10. The names are symbolic phases of a year, not fixed seasons; numeric identifiers remain definitive in date notation. Odd-numbered months have 36 days; even-numbered months have 37, except month 10 has 38 in a leap year. All months begin with Focus Day 1.

The six-day metric week

For a regular metric day d (1–36), its position in the metric week is ((d − 1) mod 6) + 1. Its metric week within the month is ceil(d / 6). For example, month day 17 is the fifth day of metric week 3: Pause (Rest Day 1).

Bonus Rest Days and Leap Day have no metric week number or metric weekday. After an extra day, the next month starts a new metric week at Begin (Focus Day 1). Gregorian weekdays continue independently; metric Rest Days do not stay on Saturday and Sunday.

Why these months and weekdays have names

Names are for people; numbers are for precision. The English-language names give the calendar a memorable rhythm, while month identifiers 01–10, weekday positions 1–6 and the date format YYYY-MM-DD remain definitive. Translating or adapting a label must never change which day a date refers to.

Why the ten months are named this way

The sequence creates a metaphorical arc through a year: renewed intention, emergence, development, contribution, reflection and restoration. It is not a claim about seasons. Northern and Southern Hemisphere climates are reversed, and tropical or equatorial regions may not follow a four-season pattern at all. Using concepts rather than weather, named rulers or particular religious observances makes the intent more portable, although the English interpretations will not feel equally natural everywhere.

Why the six weekdays are named this way

The week follows an understandable progression of action, connection and recovery. The first four positions are Focus Days; the last two are Rest Days. Instead of borrowing the names of European gods or planets, naming days for a particular faith, or importing terms from another living culture without context, these words describe possible human experiences. They are not commands: a person may work on Renew or rest on Create.

A calendar meant to cross borders, not erase differences

No English set of names is culturally universal. Different languages may require different grammatical forms or locally meaningful translations; those should be reviewed with speakers, not assumed to be literal substitutions. The Unicode CLDR guidance on date and time names illustrates why names and formats need localisation.

Nor does a six-day metric week replace existing seven-day schedules. Its Pause and Renew days shift relative to Gregorian Saturdays and Sundays. Religious observances, customary rest practices, school schedules, employment rights and public holidays retain their own rules. The practical way to explore this calendar is alongside the Gregorian one, not by silently moving anyone’s obligations.

What naming does—and does not—claim.

The names are design choices meant to aid understanding, not findings from cross-cultural user research. Broad international acceptance and claimed benefits would require testing. Bonus Rest Days and Leap Day remain outside the six named weekdays.

Bonus days, leap years, and year-end

Five Bonus Rest Days

Day 37 of every even-numbered month is a Bonus Rest Day. It belongs to that month but sits outside its six metric weeks. A common year ends with the Bonus Rest Day YYYY-10-37, which is also New Year’s Eve.

The leap-year rule

A year is a leap year if divisible by 4, except century years must also be divisible by 400. Thus 2000 and 2028 are leap years; 1900 and 2100 are common years. This is the Gregorian rule documented by the U.S. Naval Observatory.

In a leap year, month 10 ends with YYYY-10-38, designated Leap Day / New Year’s Eve. Day 37 remains a Bonus Rest Day but is no longer New Year’s Eve. Leap Day is separate from both the regular metric weeks and the five Bonus Rest Days.

Gregorian February 29 is not Metric Leap Day.

Conversion preserves the day’s position in the year. For example, g2028-02-29 = 2028-02-24, while g2028-12-31 = 2028-10-38. See the conversion method.

How to write a date

Write a Metric date as YYYY-MM-DD, without a prefix. In this project’s notation, a Gregorian date starts with a lowercase g: gYYYY-MM-DD. The prefix is a project convention, not a standard Gregorian date format.

2026-05-36
Year 2026, metric month 05 (Radiate), day 36.
g2026-07-01
Gregorian July 1, 2026: the same day.

Always label the calendar when context could be unclear. A Metric date such as 2026-02-37 is valid here but is not a valid Gregorian date for a standard date field.

The converter supports years 0001–9999, without a year zero. It applies the Gregorian rules to all supported years (the proleptic Gregorian calendar). It does not model historical local calendar changes. The year range is a tool limit, not an added calendar rule.
